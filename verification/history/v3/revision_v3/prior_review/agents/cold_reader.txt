Reconstruction and answers to the three cold-reader questions

1. Why is a sextuple impossible in the zero/one/two-small cases?
A tuple of size at least six contains a sextuple. Order it a,b,s,t,c,d by modulus, allowing ties. A small entry has modulus below three. Structural estimates first show that the last three entries of any ordered quadruple with second modulus at least three are irregular. Their fourth modulus grows at least quadratically in the preceding entries; determinant estimates and coherent interpolation strengthen the growth enough to place the final modulus in the approximation argument's usable range.

With no small entries, Lemma 2.5 gives T>3ABS. Lemma 3.7 then yields C>A²T⁵/S² and D>A²C⁷/(S²T⁴), with the separation required by every approximation family. Keeping the Vandermonde factor strengthens the first inequality to C>400A⁷B⁵S³. The explicit endpoint quotients of Section 6 exceed one. The prescribed branch on S relative to B² and B³ joins the initial interval to an infinite ordinary sequence. No finite D survives this chain.

With exactly one small entry, the same interval strategy first excludes five unbounded regions. Thus either N(a)=1 and B<8, or N(a)=2 and B<4. Apart from the rational pair (1,3), the field discriminant is at most 24 and the complete pair enumeration has forty representatives. Fixed-pair estimates exclude S≥15, leaving fifty-six triples after one already excluded triple is removed. Section 9 assigns each triple a cutoff N0. The approximation chain excludes N(t)≥N0. Structural growth excludes all of the complementary range in 38 rows; the explicit irregularity quadratic excludes 16 further rows; two remaining rows are excluded by a short norm congruence. The rational pair gives the triple (1,3,8), for which N0=1024 contradicts T>48.

With at least two small entries, the small-entry classification allows at most three. Exactly two small entries and four large ones force the third large modulus C below 10^550. For a nondegenerate small pair, reversible descent generates every common extension in that disk from finitely many seeds, and the exact compatibility graph has no triangles. The three smallest of the four large entries would therefore form an impossible triangle. The product −1 pairs are treated separately: {-1,1} by axis restrictions, a field bound, and a continued-fraction computation; the Eisenstein pair by an integral transformation and a purely imaginary-axis restriction. With three small entries, the ten classified triples have at most one further entry through 10^40. If all three further entries are larger, the vanishing/nonvanishing alternatives for coherent interpolation contradict the ordinary height bound. If one bounded entry exists, it is a fourth anchor and two overlapping forbidden intervals contradict that same upper bound. These arguments exhaust the at-least-two-small case.

2. How do the integral nonzero forms exclude intervals, and why do finite families plus an ordinary tail cover all D?
For fixed anchors and c,d, tuple roots produce an evaluation vector containing z=√(cd+1) and products of integral roots. Column divisibility ensures that every evaluated row is integral after the required anchor divisions. Full matrix rank ensures that at least one evaluated form is nonzero. In an imaginary quadratic integer ring its norm is a positive integer, hence its complex modulus is at least one. The remainder estimate and the approximation of the other square roots by root products give 1<U/Z+VZ. Here Z=|z|, and Z² differs from CD by at most one. A range in which each of U/Z and VZ is at most 1/2 is impossible, including its endpoints because the total inequality is strict. Equation (5.4) records the safe conversion.

An interval itself is bounded: sufficiently small Z makes the reciprocal term large and sufficiently large Z makes the linear term large. Thus one family cannot exclude an entire tail. The finite families are chosen to bridge different ranges. Growth puts D above the first lower endpoint. Avoidance of each closed interval then puts it above that interval's upper endpoint, which exceeds the next lower endpoint by the verified overlap quotient. The ordinary family is available at every order j. Its ratio of successive overlap quotients increases by (R/H)²>1, so one checked tail junction proves every later junction; its lower endpoints tend to infinity. Hence the fixed D would have to exceed unboundedly large endpoints. The treatment of empty intermediate intervals in lines 2004–2008 is correct and useful, not redundant.

3. How do the pair/triple lists and N0 eliminate every one-small case without a fourth-entry enumeration or He–Togbe–Ziegler?
The pair/triple lists are finite because the preceding analytic arguments bound N(a), B, the field discriminant (unless the pair is (1,3)), and S. The exact coordinate and square tests then give all forty pairs and all 57 third-entry candidates; the pair lemma discards (1,−3,3). For each of the 56 remaining triples, N0 is the maximum of the analytic-size guards and integer thresholds at which all endpoint quotients and the ordinary-tail ratio exceed one. Therefore the infinite interval argument excludes every N(t)≥N0 at once. Below N0, the inequalities in Section 9.2 exclude whole modulus ranges, rather than enumerating fourth entries. The last two rows use the norm equation u²+2v² in the narrow range 30<T<32. For (1,3), Lemma 8.2 makes every extra entry rational and rules out a sextuple with a negative entry; the resulting positive third entry is 8. The same N0 argument gives t<32, contradicting t>48. No integer-quintuple theorem enters this reasoning.
